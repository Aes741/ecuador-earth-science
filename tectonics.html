Plate Tectonics, Earthquakes, and Volcanoes

Ecuador's position on the Pacific Ring of Fire makes the country both geologically productive and exposed to natural hazards.

The big idea

A country on a moving boundary

Off Ecuador's coast, the oceanic Nazca Plate dives beneath the continental South American Plate at roughly 6–8 centimeters per year. This convergent boundary compresses and lifts the Andes, generates earthquakes, and supplies magma to the volcanic arc.

The boundary is a subduction zone, not a transform or divergent boundary. The Peru–Chile Trench marks the offshore edge of the plate system; in the Galápagos, the Galápagos hotspot and spreading center add a second setting where new oceanic crust and shield volcanoes form.

Hazard connection

The same plate motion links Ecuador's hazards: a locked megathrust can release a major earthquake and tsunami, while water released from the descending slab helps create magma for volcanoes.

Natural disasters

Earthquakes, tsunamis, and eruptions

Earthquakes

Shallow crustal faults and the offshore subduction interface both produce earthquakes. The 1906 Ecuador–Colombia earthquake was about magnitude 8.8 and generated a Pacific tsunami; the 2016 Pedernales earthquake was magnitude 7.8.

Tsunamis

Large offshore earthquakes can displace the seafloor. Ecuador's coast and the Galápagos therefore use tsunami maps, evacuation routes, sirens, and alerts coordinated by the national risk-management system.

Volcanic activity

Volcanoes in the Andes are mostly composite (stratovolcanoes) built from repeated lava flows, ash, and pyroclastic deposits. Ash, lahars, lava, and gas can threaten communities, roads, farms, and airports.

Historical earthquakes

Remembering the shaking

1797 · Riobamba

An exceptionally destructive earthquake devastated Riobamba and surrounding Andean settlements. Estimates of magnitude and deaths vary because instruments did not exist, but the event remains one of Ecuador's most important historical earthquakes.

1949 · Ambato

A powerful earthquake destroyed much of Ambato and nearby towns, killing thousands and leading to major rebuilding and disaster-response changes.

2016 · Pedernales (M 7.8)

The subduction earthquake struck Ecuador's northern coast. More than 600 people died, tens of thousands needed shelter, and roads, hospitals, homes, and schools were damaged.

Preparing citizens

Ecuador's Secretaría Nacional de Gestión de Riesgos coordinates risk maps, public alerts, evacuation drills, emergency kits, and guidance to drop, cover, and hold on. The Geophysical Institute of the Escuela Politécnica Nacional monitors earthquakes and volcanoes.

Families can strengthen buildings, secure heavy furniture, identify an outdoor meeting place, and follow official alerts rather than rumors.

Rock and mineral resources

Geology that supports livelihoods

Ecuador has gold, copper, silver, zinc, lead, limestone, pumice, clay, sand, gravel, and construction stone. Major industrial operations include the Fruta del Norte gold mine in Zamora-Chinchipe and the Mirador copper mine in the Cordillera del Cóndor. Gold and copper concentrates add export income and create direct and indirect work in engineering, transport, construction, equipment, and local services.

Recent trade data show copper ores and concentrates moving primarily to China, while gold is sold through international refining and trading centers including Switzerland and the United States. The USGS and U.S. International Trade Administration describe mining as a growing, but still smaller, part of Ecuador's diversified economy.

Trade-offs

Benefits and costs

Mining can fund public revenue, exports, and jobs, but extraction can also disturb forests, consume water, produce tailings, and create conflict over land and Indigenous rights. Illegal mining and mercury contamination are serious concerns in parts of the Amazon. Strong permits, monitoring, mine reclamation, worker protections, and community consultation are necessary if economic benefits are to last.

Mountain ranges

The Andes divide the country

Cordillera Occidental

The western Andean chain contains volcanic peaks such as Chimborazo (6,263 m), Ecuador's highest summit, and Illinizas. It forms the western wall of the highland corridor.

Cordillera Real / Oriental

The eastern chain includes Cayambe, Antisana, Cotopaxi, and El Altar. Glaciers, páramo wetlands, and snowmelt feed rivers that descend toward the Amazon.

Avenue of Volcanoes

Between the two cordilleras, a long inter-Andean valley holds Quito, Latacunga, Riobamba, and many farms. Alexander von Humboldt gave this volcanic landscape its famous name.

Galápagos highlands

On the islands, broad shield volcanoes rise from the ocean floor. Their lava landscapes and endemic species support conservation, research, and tourism.

Weather and water

The Andes force moist air upward, creating wetter slopes and rain shadows. Páramo grasslands store water like a sponge and release it gradually to cities, farms, and hydropower. Elevation also creates many climate zones within short distances.

Culture and movement

High valleys supported Indigenous agriculture and connected Quito, Cuenca, and other communities. Mountain passes shaped Inca and colonial routes; today highways and tunnels cross the same barriers, while landslides and eruptions can still interrupt trade.

Volcano field guide

Fire beneath the Andes

Cotopaxi · 5,897 m

A steep, ice-capped composite volcano and one of the world's highest active volcanoes. Ash and lahars are the main threats; its symmetrical cone also attracts climbers and visitors.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons, Cotopaxi Volcano.

Tungurahua · 5,023 m

A composite volcano near Baños with repeated explosive eruptions, especially from 1999–2016. Ash and lava flows affected farms, roads, and tourism, while hot springs and scenery support the local economy.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons, Volcán Tungurahua.

Reventador · 3,562 m

An active composite volcano east of the Andes. Its remote location limits some exposure, but ashfall and pyroclastic flows can affect the Amazon-facing region and transport routes.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons, Reventador volcano.

Galápagos shields

Isabela, Fernandina, and other islands are broad shield volcanoes built by fluid basaltic lava. Eruptions can alter habitats, yet volcanic geology helps create the landscapes that draw tourists and researchers.

Photo: Wikimedia Commons, Fernandina Island.
