Weathering, Mass Wasting, and Erosion

Ecuador's steep Andes, humid Amazon slopes, dry coastal forests, and active volcanoes create a natural laboratory for external Earth processes.

Weathering

Water changes the rock before gravity moves it.

Warm, wet conditions accelerate chemical weathering in Ecuador. Carbonic acid in rainwater dissolves carbonate minerals, while hydrolysis changes feldspar in volcanic and granitic rocks into clay minerals. In the Andes, repeated wetting, drying, and temperature changes weaken volcanic ash, lava, and soil. Roots and burrowing organisms add biological weathering.

Once rock and soil are weakened, intense rainfall can trigger shallow landslides, debris flows, and rockfalls. Steep slopes, roads cut into hillsides, deforestation, and El Niño-related rainfall can increase the hazard.

Key idea

Weathering breaks material down in place; mass wasting moves that material downslope under gravity. In Ecuador the two processes often work together during a period of intense rain.

Mass wasting case study

Rainfall, slopes, and the 2023 Alausí landslide

On March 26, 2023, a large landslide struck the community of Alausí in Chimborazo Province after a period of heavy rain. The failure destroyed homes and roads and caused many deaths and evacuations. The event shows how saturated soil, weak volcanic materials, steep relief, and settlement on unstable slopes can combine into a disaster.

The government response included evacuations, search and rescue, temporary shelter, road and utility work, and continued slope monitoring. Longer-term risk reduction includes hazard mapping, land-use planning, rainfall monitoring, evacuation routes, and the National Multi-Hazard Early Warning System coordinated by SNGR and technical agencies such as INAMHI. Water erosion

Rivers connect the Andes to the Pacific and Amazon.

High-gradient headwaters commonly have narrow, straight or gently sinuous channels that cut downward. On flatter floodplains, rivers meander, migrate laterally, and deposit sediment; braided reaches form where sediment load is high and banks are easily eroded.

Río Napo

Begins on the eastern slopes of the Andes, flows through Ecuador and Peru, and joins the Amazon. It is about 1,130 km long; a representative discharge near Francisco de Orellana is about 1,100 m³/s, varying with season and station. Its lower reaches are mainly meandering and support river transport, fishing, farming, and Indigenous communities.

Photo source ↗

Río Guayas

Forms where the Daule and Babahoyo rivers meet near Guayaquil. Its roughly 60-km main stem opens into the Gulf of Guayaquil and a tidal estuary/delta; discharge near the mouth is roughly 2,000–2,200 m³/s in published estimates. The basin is Ecuador's most important coastal drainage system for ports, agriculture, fisheries, and urban development.

Photo source ↗

Río Esmeraldas

Begins as the Guayllabamba system in the northern Andes and runs about 320 km to the Pacific. Published estimates place discharge near the mouth around 300–400 m³/s, with strong seasonal variation. Its lower, meandering valley carries sediment to a coastal estuary and supports farms, settlements, fisheries, and the port city of Esmeraldas.

A river in rapid change

The Coca River erosion crisis

The collapse of the San Rafael lava-dam waterfall in 2020 caused unusually rapid headward (regressive) erosion on the Río Coca. The river cut new canyons, destabilized banks, moved enormous quantities of sediment, and threatened roads, pipelines, communities, and the Coca Codo Sinclair hydropower system. Ecuadorian agencies and the USGS have monitored channel change, streamflow, and sediment to guide risk reduction.

Economic and social effects: Bank erosion removes farmland and can damage bridges, roads, homes, and water intakes. Floodplain sediment can renew agricultural soil, but floods and channel migration also displace residents. In the Guayas estuary, sedimentation increases dredging needs and affects ports; along the Napo, erosion and flooding affect transport, fishing, and river-dependent communities.
Wind, drylands, and desertification

Ecuador has drylands, but not a vast erg desert.

Wind erosion is most likely in exposed, sparsely vegetated parts of the Santa Elena Peninsula, southern coastal dry forests, and high inter-Andean valleys during drought. It removes fine soil, dries crops, and adds dust. Ecuador's main erosion problem is water erosion, but vegetation loss, overgrazing, fires, and poor land management can make wind and water erosion worse.

The country does not contain a large Sahara-like desert. Dry landscapes include the Santa Elena dry region, with coastal scrub and sandy surfaces, and the Palmira highland landscape near Guamote, where wind-shaped sandy soils and sparse vegetation resemble a high-altitude desert. These are better described as drylands than as classic erg (sand-sea), reg (gravel plain), or hamada (rock plateau).

Government and FAO-supported responses include restoring dry forests, protecting vegetation, improving grazing and agricultural practices, monitoring land degradation, and applying sustainable land management in dry and semi-arid areas.

Mitigation checklist
  • Keep ground covered with native vegetation.
  • Use contour farming, terraces, and windbreaks.
  • Protect riparian vegetation and wetlands.
  • Use rainfall, river, and slope monitoring for alerts.
  • Keep new construction away from unstable slopes and active channels.
Glaciers and ancient ice

Retreating ice still shapes water and hazards.

Current glaciers

Glaciers remain on high volcanoes including Chimborazo, Cotopaxi, Cayambe, Antisana, and El Altar. Measurements and repeat imagery show substantial retreat, especially on Chimborazo and Cotopaxi. Meltwater contributes to high-Andean streams and reservoirs, although glacier loss eventually reduces this dry-season buffer.

Glacial features

Cirques, U-shaped valleys, moraines, and glacial lakes record older ice. These features influence tourism, highland ecosystems, water storage, and the location of roads and farms. Unstable moraine dams and steep volcanic slopes can contribute to outburst-flood and debris-flow risk.

Research in action

The World Glacier Monitoring Service and Ecuadorian researchers use field measurements, aerial photographs, and satellite images to track ice change. These records help communities plan for changing water supply and volcanic or glacial hazards.
